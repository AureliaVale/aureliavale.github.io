How to Write Grief in Fiction

Grief is one of the most written-about experiences in literary fiction, and one of the most frequently written badly.

The failure usually comes from reaching too quickly for the large emotion — the tears, the collapse, the dramatic outpouring. But grief, in life, is rarely like this. It is more often ordinary. It arrives in supermarkets, at traffic lights, in the middle of a sentence about something completely unrelated. It is disproportionate and then absent and then overwhelming again.

To write grief well, write the specific, small, unexpected moments. The bereaved character who cannot stop noticing that the dead person will never see this particular thing again — a season, a film, a grandchild. The way grief makes the world look different, as if a filter has been placed over everything.

And resist resolution. Grief does not resolve. It becomes part of the texture of a life. A novel that ends with grief "healed" has not told the truth.

The Deeper Story was written for writers who want to work at this depth.
