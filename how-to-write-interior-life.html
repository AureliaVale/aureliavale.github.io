How to Write Interior Life: What Your Character Is Really Thinking

Interior life — the rendering of a character's inner experience — is where literary fiction lives or dies.

It is also where many writers go wrong. The most common failure is summary: telling the reader what a character feels rather than rendering the experience of feeling it. "She was sad" is not interior life. It is a report. Interior life is the texture of sadness — what it feels like from inside, in this body, in this moment, with these specific associations.

The second failure is overcrowding. Interior life should not be constant. A character who is always reflecting, always processing, always articulating their feelings becomes exhausting. The most powerful interior moments arrive in contrast — after a stretch of action or dialogue, a sudden stillness in which the full weight of what has just happened is felt.

And interior life must be specific to this character. Their thoughts should sound like no one else's.

The Deeper Story was written for writers who want to work at this depth.
