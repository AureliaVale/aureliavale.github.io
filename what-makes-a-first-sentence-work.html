What Makes a First Sentence Work?

The first sentence of a novel has one job: to make the reader read the second sentence. But in literary fiction, it carries something more — a whole atmosphere, a whole way of seeing, compressed into a few words.

Consider the famous openings that have endured. They share certain qualities: a particular voice, an immediate tension, a world already in motion. They do not explain. They do not introduce. They simply begin, and in beginning, they commit — to a tone, a register, a way of looking at the world that the rest of the novel will have to sustain.

The first sentence is a promise. Not a promise about what will happen, but about how the story will feel to read. A first sentence that promises wit and delivers solemnity has broken faith with its reader. A first sentence that promises silence and delivers noise has done the same.

Write your first sentence last. Or write it first, then rewrite it a hundred times. Either way, do not leave it until it is completely, absolutely right.
