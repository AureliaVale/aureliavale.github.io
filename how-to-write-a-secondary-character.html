How to Write a Secondary Character Who Feels Real

Secondary characters fail in two opposite directions: they are either too thin — present only to serve the plot, with no inner life of their own — or too thick, so fully developed that they overwhelm the story they are supposed to support.

The key is selective depth. A secondary character does not need a complete backstory. They need one or two specific qualities that make them feel like a person — a particular way of speaking, a habit, a contradiction between what they say and what they do.

And they need their own agenda. Even a character who appears in three scenes should want something, even if what they want is small and never quite achieved. Characters without desires are furniture.

The test of a well-written secondary character is whether the reader wonders, briefly, what their life looks like when they are not in this scene.

The Deeper Story was written for writers who want to work at this depth.
