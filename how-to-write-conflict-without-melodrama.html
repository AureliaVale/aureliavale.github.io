How to Write a Scene of Conflict Without Making It Melodramatic

Conflict in literary fiction is rarely the raised voices, the slammed doors, the dramatic confrontation of commercial storytelling. It is more often quiet, compressed, and almost invisible to those outside the scene — which is precisely what makes it devastating.

The melodramatic scene tells the reader everything: every emotion is named, every wound is displayed, every reaction is large. The literary scene withholds. It gives the reader a conversation about something ordinary — dinner, a journey, a piece of news — while the real conflict runs underneath, never quite surfacing, pressing against the surface from below.

The key is specificity of behaviour. How does this particular person behave when they are in pain? Some go cold. Some become precise. Some retreat into excessive politeness. These behaviours are more revealing than any outburst — and more true.

The Deeper Story was written for writers who want to work at this depth.
